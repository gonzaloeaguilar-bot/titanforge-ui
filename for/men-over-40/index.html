Can Men Over 40 Still Build Muscle?

Yes. Resistance training adds lean mass well past 40, and the research on adults over 50 shows it. A 2011 meta-analysis in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise (Peterson and colleagues, PMID 20543750) pooled 49 studies with 1,328 participants aged 50 and older. Resistance training increased lean body mass by an average of 1.1 kilograms. Higher-volume programs produced larger gains, and the oldest participants gained somewhat less.

Two practical points follow. Starting earlier is better than waiting, and the amount of work you do across a week matters. Recovery, joint tolerance, sleep and stress decide how much of that work you can repeat, which is where a plan built for you does better than a program written for a 25-year-old.

How Much Protein Should A Man Over 40 Eat?

Around 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight a day is a well-supported target. A 2018 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (Morton and colleagues, PMID 28698222) pooled 49 trials with 1,863 participants. Protein supplementation increased gains in strength and fat-free mass during resistance training, but total protein intake beyond 1.62 grams per kilogram per day produced no further gain in fat-free mass. The benefit of extra protein also became smaller with age.

For a man weighing 90 kilograms, 1.62 grams per kilogram is about 146 grams of protein a day, from food first and a shake when food falls short. Anyone with kidney disease or on a prescribed diet should check with their doctor before raising protein intake.

Why the research looks at adults over 50

Most trials on age and muscle recruit adults over 50 or over 65, because that is where loss of muscle with age is measured most clearly. Men in their 40s sit between those groups and younger trained lifters. The direction of the evidence is the same in both: training volume and adequate protein matter, and recovery needs more attention as you get older.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is training over 40 different?

The principles are the same, but recovery, joint tolerance, stress, sleep, and warm-up quality often need more respect.

Can men over 40 still build muscle?

Yes, but the plan needs progressive training, adequate protein, recovery, and consistency instead of random hard sessions that cannot be repeated.

Should fat loss be slower after 40?

Often, a moderate approach is smarter because aggressive cuts can hurt performance, recovery, mood, and adherence. The right pace depends on the person.

What matters most for long-term strength?

Good exercise selection, repeatable technique, progressive overload, recovery signals, and not confusing soreness with progress.

When should someone over 40 talk to a doctor first?

Before starting if they have medical conditions, symptoms, injuries, medications, or concerns that require clinical clearance.
