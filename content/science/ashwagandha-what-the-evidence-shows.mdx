Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) modestly reduces perceived stress and cortisol in chronically stressed lifters, providing minor secondary benefits for recovery and endurance. It is not an anabolic agent. Take 300 to 600 mg of standardized root extract daily.

In the fitness industry, adaptogens are routinely marketed as near-miraculous botanical compounds capable of simultaneously erasing adrenal fatigue, melting visceral fat, and sending endogenous testosterone levels through the roof. Most of these claims collapse the moment you pull the primary literature. Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*), an evergreen shrub with centuries of traditional use in Ayurvedic medicine, is currently the most popular of these botanicals.

When trainees come to us asking whether they should add ashwagandha to their daily stack, they are usually looking for a pharmacological shortcut around high life stress, poor sleep hygiene, or excessive training volume. My role as an exercise physiologist is to separate physiological mechanism from marketing exaggeration. We do not guess, and we do not accept promotional claims from supplement distributors. We examine randomized human trials, evaluate statistical effect sizes, and establish whether an intervention produces meaningful real-world adaptations.

Let us examine the bioactive chemistry of *Withania somnifera*, analyze the clinical evidence on stress, cognition, and exercise performance, define its genuine limitations, and outline a practical dosing strategy.

## Botanical Chemistry and Pharmacological Mechanisms

The therapeutic properties of *Withania somnifera* root are primarily attributed to a class of naturally occurring steroidal lactones known as withanolides, most notably withaferin A and withanolide A, along with various alkaloids and sitoindosides.

In human physiology, chronic psychological or physiological stress stimulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The hypothalamus secretes corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which prompts the anterior pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), ultimately triggering the adrenal cortex to produce cortisol. While acute cortisol elevation is an essential survival response and a natural byproduct of intense resistance training, chronically elevated basal cortisol drives skeletal muscle protein breakdown, impairs sleep architecture, suppresses immune function, and disrupts autonomic recovery.

The primary bioactive mechanisms of standardized ashwagandha extract involve:

1. **HPA Axis Attenuation:** Withanolides appear to downregulate excessive CRH and ACTH signaling in individuals undergoing sustained stress, leading to measurable reductions in circulating serum and salivary cortisol concentrations.
2. **GABAergic Neurotransmission Modulation:** Ashwagandha demonstrates gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-mimetic activity. By binding to or modulating GABA-A receptors in the central nervous system, withanolide constituents promote inhibitory neural signaling, reducing sympathetic nervous system hyperarousal, subjective anxiety, and somatic tension.
3. **Antioxidant and Mitochondrial Support:** Bioactive withanolides upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes—such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase—mitigating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in neural and muscular tissues under metabolic stress.

These physiological actions explain why ashwagandha functions predominantly as a mild calming agent and adaptogen rather than an aggressive ergogenic stimulant.

## Neuropsychiatric and Clinical Evidence: The D'Cruz et al. Review

To evaluate the broader clinical validity of *Withania somnifera*, D'Cruz and Andrade (PMID 36062480) conducted a comprehensive review titled "Potential clinical applications of Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera) in medicine and neuropsychiatry" published in *Expert Review of Clinical Pharmacology*.

D'Cruz and Andrade reviewed studies of ashwagandha in animals and humans across several categories, including stress, anxiety disorders, sleep quality, and cognition. Their review established several critical findings:

- **Stress and Anxiety Reductions:** In human subjects, the review reports that ashwagandha enhances adaptogenesis in healthy adults and modestly benefits generalized anxiety disorder and obsessive-compulsive disorder.
- **Stress Markers:** The review reports that ashwagandha attenuates laboratory and clinical indices of stress.
- **Sleep Quality Improvements:** The review reports that ashwagandha appears to improve sleep quality, and it suggests that modulation of GABA and glutamate may be part of how its benefits arise.
- **Methodological Nuance:** D'Cruz and Andrade cautioned that reports of clinical benefit must be interpreted with caution given the paucity of randomized clinical trials, small numbers of patients, and relatively short treatment periods.

The review by D'Cruz and Andrade suggests that ashwagandha may confer modest benefit in certain neuropsychiatric conditions, but it emphasizes that greater methodological rigor is needed before clinical recommendations can be made confidently.

## Cognitive Function and Mood: The Clinical Trial by Leonard et al.

Beyond general stress reduction, how does ashwagandha supplementation influence acute cognitive performance, mental clarity, and mood states during demanding tasks?

This question was directly investigated by Leonard and colleagues (PMID 38931168) in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in *Nutrients* titled "Acute and Repeated Ashwagandha Supplementation Improves Markers of Cognitive Function and Mood."

Leonard and co-authors evaluated the effects of both single-dose (acute) and sustained 30-day (repeated) supplementation with a standardized *Withania somnifera* extract in healthy men and women. The investigators assessed psychomotor speed, executive function, sustained attention, and subjective mood scales.

The trial conducted by Leonard and colleagues yielded several relevant outcomes:

- **Attention and Memory:** Participants receiving the extract improved acute and/or 30-day measures of word recall, choice reaction time, and digit vigilance from baseline more consistently, with several differences observed between groups.
- **Acute vs. Chronic Adaptations:** Participants were retested 60 minutes after a single dose and again after 30 days of daily use, and the trial reports improvements in acute and/or 30-day measures.
- **Mood Profile Stabilization:** Leonard and colleagues reported lower perceptions of tension and fatigue on the profile of mood states.

These findings from Leonard and colleagues suggest that ashwagandha (225 mg) may improve some measures of memory, attention, vigilance, and executive function while decreasing perceptions of tension and fatigue in younger healthy individuals.

## Exercise Endurance and Cardiorespiratory Capacity: The Prajapati et al. Trial

A growing area of athletic interest is whether ashwagandha enhances physical performance and cardiorespiratory endurance in active populations.

This was rigorously examined by Prajapati and colleagues (PMID 41846233) in a randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial titled "Efficacy and Safety of Low-Dose Ashwagandha Supplementation on Exercise Endurance: A Randomized, Placebo-Controlled, Double-Blind, Clinical Trial" published in *Phytotherapy Research*.

Prajapati and co-authors investigated the impact of low-dose ashwagandha (30 mg per day) over eight weeks in healthy adults. The investigators measured VO2 max (estimated with a treadmill test), lactic acid, creatine phosphokinase, perceived exertion, fatigue scores, and safety biomarkers.

The trial by Prajapati and colleagues reported notable performance outcomes:

- **Cardiorespiratory Endurance (VO2 Max):** Subjects supplementing with low-dose ashwagandha showed significantly improved estimated VO2 max and maximal heart rate compared to the placebo cohort.
- **Lower Fatigue Markers:** The active group showed lower lactic acid levels at exercise, reduced creatine phosphokinase, and improved perceived exertion and fatigue scores.
- **Safety and Tolerability:** Prajapati and colleagues reported that hematological, hepatic, renal, and metabolic parameters stayed within normal clinical ranges and that the extract was well tolerated, with no treatment-related adverse events over eight weeks in healthy adults.

The work of Prajapati and colleagues suggests that low-dose ashwagandha has potential as a safe aid for endurance capacity in healthy adults over eight weeks.

## What Ashwagandha Does NOT Do: Dismantling the Hype

To evaluate ashwagandha objectively, we must strip away the unsubstantiated marketing narratives that dominate social media and retail supplement stores.

### 1. It Is Not an Anabolic Steroid or Primary Muscle Builder
You will frequently see advertisements claiming ashwagandha is a natural muscle builder that skyrockets testosterone. While a few isolated studies in untrained or highly stressed men have noted slight increases in total testosterone (typically 10 to 15 percent), this is an indirect byproduct of reducing high baseline cortisol, not direct androgenic stimulation. In healthy, well-nourished lifters with normal baseline endocrine function, ashwagandha will not produce meaningful changes in free testosterone or directly stimulate myofibrillar protein synthesis.

### 2. It Does Not Replace Sleep, Recovery, or Intelligent Programming
Taking 600 mg of ashwagandha root extract will not offset the systemic damage of sleeping five hours per night, drinking excessive alcohol, or maintaining an unsustainable weekly training volume. Adaptogens do not eliminate biological fatigue; they merely modulate stress perception. If your lifestyle fundamentals are broken, no botanical root will rescue your performance.

### 3. It Does Not Burn Body Fat or Accelerate Metabolic Rate
Ashwagandha is not a thermogenic agent. It contains no sympathomimetic compounds like caffeine, synephrine, or yohimbine. Any fat loss reported in clinical trials involving stressed cohorts is mediated by reduced stress-induced emotional eating and improved sleep quality, not direct lipolysis. Energy balance remains the non-negotiable determinant of fat loss.

## Safety, Tolerability, and Adverse Effect Profile

While standardized *Withania somnifera* extracts exhibit a favorable safety profile in the published literature, it is not free of potential side effects and contraindications.

1. **Gastrointestinal Discomfort:** Mild nausea, diarrhea, or upset stomach can occur, particularly when taking high doses on an empty stomach. Consuming the supplement with food easily resolves this issue.
2. **Thyroid Hormone Modulation:** Ashwagandha can mildly stimulate the thyroid gland, increasing circulating levels of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Individuals diagnosed with hyperthyroidism or Graves' disease must avoid ashwagandha, and anyone taking exogenous thyroid medication should exercise caution.
3. **Sedation and Lethargy:** Because of its GABA-mimetic activity, some individuals experience daytime grogginess or an unwanted blunting of emotional arousal (emotional flattening) when taking high doses or using it during daytime hours.
4. **Idiosyncratic Liver Concerns:** Extremely rare case reports of acute, reversible drug-induced liver injury have been reported in international medical databases, typically associated with non-standardized proprietary blends, multi-ingredient formulations, or contaminated botanical raw materials. Purchasing verified, third-party tested standardized extracts is essential.

## Dosing Protocol and Extract Selection

When selecting an ashwagandha product, raw unstandardized root powder is inefficient due to wild variability in active withanolide concentrations. You must look for standardized root extracts that clearly state their withanolide content on the label.

### 1. Extract Standardization
Look for standardized root extracts such as KSM-66 (standardized to 5% withanolides) or Sensoril (standardized to 10% withanolides). These extracts are produced via standardized extraction procedures that guarantee chemical consistency across batches.

### 2. Daily Dosing
- **Standardized Root Extract (e.g., KSM-66):** 300 to 600 mg per day, taken in a single dose or split into two 300 mg doses.
- **High-Concentration Extract (e.g., Sensoril):** 125 to 250 mg once or twice daily.

### 3. Timing of Ingestion
Because ashwagandha modulates GABAergic pathways and promotes relaxation, most trainees benefit from taking their daily dose with dinner or roughly 30 to 60 minutes before bed. This timing optimizes sleep latency while preventing daytime sluggishness.

### 4. Duration and Cycling
Given that the vast majority of clinical trials evaluate periods of 8 to 12 weeks, a prudent protocol is to use ashwagandha during periods of acute lifestyle or training stress (such as a rigorous training block, a caloric deficit, or a high-workload professional phase) for 8 to 12 weeks, followed by a 2 to 4-week washout period.

## What the Evidence Does Not Support

A rigorous scientific appraisal requires highlighting where current literature is incomplete:

- **The evidence does not support indefinite, year-round usage:** Long-term safety data beyond six months of continuous daily use is limited. Cycling off periodically remains the most sensible approach.
- **The evidence does not support acute pre-workout performance boosts:** Unlike caffeine or beta-alanine, ashwagandha does not provide an immediate surge in force production or central drive when taken 30 minutes before stepping under a barbell.
- **The evidence does not support direct hypertrophy enhancement in trained lifters:** Any observed hypertrophic benefit in studies is secondary to improved recovery and training consistency in stressed populations, not direct intracellular signaling through the mTOR pathway.

## Monday Morning Implementation Protocol

If you choose to incorporate ashwagandha into your routine, follow this protocol:

1. **Audit your recovery fundamentals first:** Confirm that you are averaging at least 7 to 9 hours of quality sleep, hitting your daily protein target, and managing total training stress before purchasing any adaptogen.
2. **Select a standardized extract:** Purchase a standalone product standardized to 5% withanolides (such as KSM-66) from a third-party tested brand. Avoid multi-ingredient proprietary blends.
3. **Dose 300 to 600 mg daily with food:** Take your dose with your evening meal or 45 minutes prior to sleep to support nighttime relaxation and minimize gastrointestinal irritation.
4. **Use for defined 8 to 12-week cycles:** Deploy the supplement during intense training blocks or phases of elevated work stress, followed by a 2 to 4-week break.
5. **Track objective recovery metrics:** Monitor resting heart rate, sleep quality, and gym performance in your training log to evaluate whether the compound delivers a tangible return on investment.

## FAQ

### How long does it take for ashwagandha to start working?

In our coaching practice, clients often expect overnight results, but meaningful adaptogenic shifts require cumulative dosing. While acute calming effects can occur within hours due to GABA-A receptor modulation, measurable reductions in resting cortisol and perceived stress typically take two to four weeks of consistent daily intake. If you do not notice changes in your recovery markers or subjective tension by week four, continuing the same protocol is unlikely to yield sudden benefits.

### Will ashwagandha make me feel emotionally numb or unmotivated?

Emotional blunting or anhedonia is one of the most common complaints we hear from lifters using high doses continuously. Because bioactive withanolides enhance inhibitory GABAergic signaling and suppress cortisol, excessive or prolonged suppression can dampen emotional arousal, drive, and training aggression. If you begin feeling indifferent or flat in the gym, drop your daily dose immediately or begin a two-week washout phase.

### Can I take ashwagandha with my morning pre-workout or caffeine?

we do not recommend combining ashwagandha directly with high-stimulant pre-workouts. Caffeine acts as an adenosine antagonist and central nervous system stimulant, whereas withanolides promote inhibitory GABAergic signaling, meaning the two compounds work in direct pharmacological opposition. If you prefer morning dosing, separate your adaptogen from your pre-workout by several hours to avoid blunting the ergogenic drive needed for heavy lifting.

### Should I take ashwagandha on rest days?

Yes, you should maintain your daily dose on non-training days without interruption. Adaptogenic compounds rely on steady tissue saturation and chronic modulation of the HPA axis rather than acute timing around training sessions. Taking your standard dose with your evening meal on rest days maintains circulating withanolide concentrations and supports parasympathetic recovery when systemic repair is highest.
