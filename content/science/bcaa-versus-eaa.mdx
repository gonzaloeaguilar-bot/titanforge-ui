Before buying BCAAs or EAAs, check what protein your meals already provide: a short-term rise in muscle protein synthesis does not establish extra muscle growth from adding another supplement.

BCAAs are leucine, isoleucine and valine, three of the nine essential amino acids. An EAA product supplies the essential set; complete protein supplies those amino acids too. These are different products, but the label alone cannot tell you which will improve your results.

## Do BCAAs actually stimulate muscle protein synthesis?

Yes, in a small human exercise study. [Jackman and colleagues (2017, PMID 28638350)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5461297/) compared a BCAA drink with placebo in ten resistance-trained young men. After a leg workout, muscle protein synthesis over four hours was 22% higher with BCAAs.

That is a real finding, not evidence of 22% more muscle growth. The trial measured a short-term synthesis rate, not months of muscle gain, recovery or strength. It also had no whey or EAA comparison group. It therefore cannot establish which supplement builds more muscle, or whether adding BCAAs to someone's usual diet improves their long-term results.

<details>
<summary>Read the study conditions</summary>

The participants received 5.6 g of BCAAs after exercise. They ate a standardized high-protein breakfast before the workout; this was not an entirely fasted training experiment. The amount describes the experiment and is not a recommended daily dose. The paper's comparisons with whey refer to earlier research, not a head-to-head test within this trial.

## Does adding amino acids make a small protein serving equivalent to a larger one?

A second acute study gives a more specific answer. [Churchward-Venne and colleagues (2012, PMID 22451437)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3424729/) studied 24 young men given either 25 g of whey, 6.25 g of whey with added leucine, or 6.25 g of whey with other essential amino acids added but no extra leucine.

Muscle protein synthesis rose early after feeding in all groups. During hours three to five after exercise, only the 25 g whey group remained significantly above its fasted rate. The early absence of a significant treatment difference does not prove the drinks were equivalent.

This experiment tested particular mixtures containing whey. It was not a comparison of every commercial EAA powder with whole food, and it did not measure long-term muscle growth. The serving sizes are study conditions, not a universal protein prescription.

## What should guide a purchase?

Start with the practical gap you need to fill. If meals are missing a convenient protein source, compare foods and complete protein powders before paying for a separate amino acid product. If appetite, dietary restrictions or a medical condition make eating difficult, get advice tailored to that problem.

The studies above support neither “BCAAs do nothing” nor “everyone needs EAAs.” They also do not establish that adding either product to an already adequate diet produces zero benefit in every circumstance. Long-term benefit, cost and convenience are separate questions from an acute laboratory response.

## FAQ

### Are BCAAs the same thing as EAAs?

No. BCAAs are three members of the essential amino acid group. An EAA formulation is intended to provide all nine. Check the actual ingredient list rather than treating the names as interchangeable.

### Does 22% higher muscle protein synthesis mean 22% more muscle?

No. The Jackman trial measured synthesis over four hours after one exercise session. It did not measure a percentage increase in muscle size over a training program.

### Should I add EAAs to a protein shake?

These studies do not establish an additional long-term benefit from that combination. Review the protein you already consume and the specific problem the extra product would solve before buying it.
