## The Volume Dose-Response Curve

Muscular hypertrophy is fundamentally an adaptive response to mechanical tension. When muscle fibers experience high mechanical loads through active lengthening and shortening, mechanosensors trigger intracellular signaling pathways that increase muscle protein synthesis.

To trigger that adaptation, how many sets do you need?

The modern evidence base provides a clear picture of the dose-response curve.

### What Schoenfeld et al. Found About Weekly Volume

The baseline for our modern understanding of weekly volume was quantified by Schoenfeld and colleagues (PMID 27433992). In their systematic review and meta-analysis titled "Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass," Schoenfeld and co-authors evaluated 15 studies to determine how weekly set count influenced muscle size.

The researchers categorized weekly volume per muscle into three brackets:
1. Low volume: fewer than 5 sets per week
2. Moderate volume: 5 to 9 sets per week
3. High volume: 10 or more sets per week

Schoenfeld and colleagues identified a graded dose-response relationship. Each additional weekly set was associated with an increase in effect size of 0.023, corresponding to about 0.37 percent more muscle gain, and higher-volume groups gained about 3.9 percent more than lower-volume groups. The three-bracket comparison (fewer than 5, 5 to 9, and 10 or more sets per muscle) showed only a trend (P = 0.074).

This meta-analysis confirmed that performing 10 or more sets per muscle group per week resulted in superior muscle gains compared to sub-10 set protocols. However, it left critical questions unanswered: Does muscle growth keep increasing linearly if you do 20, 30, or 40 sets? Where does the ceiling sit?

### What Pelland et al. (PMID 41343037) Found About Diminishing Returns

To resolve the non-linear nature of this dose-response relationship, Pelland and colleagues (PMID 41343037) conducted an extensive meta-regression titled "The Resistance Training Dose Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains."

Pelland and co-authors ran multilevel meta-regressions on 67 studies. Their findings indicated that the relationship between weekly sets and muscle hypertrophy shows diminishing returns:

- **Gains rise with volume:** The posterior probability that volume has a positive effect on hypertrophy was 100%, indicating that gains in muscle size increase as volume increases.
- **Diminishing returns:** The best-fit models suggest diminishing returns as volume rises, with the diminishing returns for strength considerably more pronounced than for hypertrophy.
- **No set count is given:** The abstract does not report a specific number of weekly sets at which the curve flattens, so any such cut-off here is a practical guideline rather than a finding of this paper.

In practical terms, Pelland and colleagues reported that gains in muscle size increase as weekly volume increases, but with diminishing returns. When you factor in the recovery cost, piling on excess sets often compromises the load and quality you can maintain on subsequent workouts.

## Proximity to Failure: Why Set Quality Dictates Volume Requirements

A critical flaw in most volume discussions is treating all sets as equal. Ten sets performed with high effort near muscular failure produce a completely different biological signal than ten sets stopped five reps short of failure.

This relationship was systematically analyzed by Robinson and colleagues (PMID 38970765) in their meta-regression, "Exploring the Dose-Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy."

Robinson and co-authors evaluated how proximity to failure, quantified as estimated Reps in Reserve (RIR), relates to strength gain and muscle hypertrophy.

The exploratory conclusions from Robinson and colleagues point to two considerations:

1. **The threshold of effective stimulus:** Robinson and colleagues found that muscle hypertrophy increased as sets were terminated closer to failure, while the relationship between proximity to failure and strength gain was negligible.
2. **The fatigue penalty of absolute failure:** While training to absolute failure (0 RIR) creates high mechanical tension, it produces disproportionate central and local fatigue compared to stopping 1 to 2 reps in reserve. Robinson and colleagues cautioned that the exact relationship between RIR and hypertrophy remains unclear, because RIR had to be estimated from study descriptions and the analysis was exploratory.

If you train at 1 to 2 RIR, you can complete 12 to 15 high-quality sets per week and recover. If you push every set to absolute concentric failure with forced reps, your central nervous system and tendons will limit your recoverable volume to a much lower threshold.

## Per-Session Volume Caps and Frequency Distribution

A related question is how to distribute your weekly sets across days.

Can you perform all 16 weekly sets for your chest on Monday afternoon?

The physiological data indicates that per-session volume has an upper threshold. After approximately 6 to 8 hard sets for a single muscle group in one session, the muscle protein synthetic signaling response becomes saturated. Sets performed beyond that threshold in the same workout suffer from declining force output and increased muscle damage without adding meaningful growth stimulus.

For optimal results:
- If a muscle group requires **6 to 8 sets per week**, you can complete them in a single session or split them across two days.
- If a muscle group requires **10 to 18 sets per week**, divide the volume across two or three weekly sessions (for instance, 6 to 8 sets on Monday and 6 to 8 sets on Thursday).

Distributing volume prevents junk volume and allows you to approach each working set with full neural drive and higher absolute loads. ## What the Evidence Does Not Support

Part of scientific rigor is acknowledging what the data does not show and where existing research falls short:

- **The evidence does not support extreme volume protocols (25+ weekly sets) for natural lifters.** While a few isolated acute trials noted minor increases with very high volumes, broader meta-regressions like Pelland et al. (PMID 41343037) indicate diminishing returns as weekly volume rises.
- **The evidence does not support single-set HIT (High Intensity Training) as optimal for experienced lifters.** Schoenfeld and colleagues (PMID 27433992) found that higher weekly volumes produced greater gains in muscle size, although their abstract does not report results by training status.
- **The literature has significant blind spots.** Most volume studies run for 6 to 12 weeks and focus on isolated muscle groups like the quadriceps and biceps. Long-term multi-year data examining back musculature, hamstrings, and older populations remains limited. We must be cautious about generalizing knee-extension quad data to compound deadlifts or heavy rows.

## Practical Programming Framework for Monday Morning

To apply these findings to your current routine, use this 4-tier volume framework:

1. **Maintenance Volume (4 to 6 sets per week):** Use this range during demanding work travel or high-stress periods to retain existing muscle mass with minimal time investment.
2. **Minimum Effective Volume (8 to 10 sets per week):** The starting point for growth. High return on investment with low fatigue. Excellent for secondary muscle groups or trainees with limited recovery bandwidth.
3. **Adaptive Target Volume (12 to 16 sets per week):** The sweet spot for maximizing hypertrophy for primary target muscles. Best distributed over 2 to 3 sessions per week.
4. **Maximum Recoverable Volume (18 to 22 sets per week):** The upper ceiling. Reserve this for specialized muscle focus blocks lasting 4 to 6 weeks, provided your sleep, caloric intake, and joint health are fully optimized.

Track your working sets in a logbook. If your performance on key exercises is increasing week over week at 12 sets, you do not need more volume. Add sets only when progress stalls, not because you think more fatigue equals more growth.

## FAQ

### What does PMID 41343037 show about diminishing returns in weekly set volume?

The meta-regression in PMID 41343037 (Pelland et al.) reported that gains in muscle size increase as weekly volume increases, but with diminishing returns. The abstract does not give a specific set count at which returns flatten, or data on fatigue and joint stress.

### At what weekly set count do diminishing returns begin for muscle hypertrophy?

Diminishing returns begin to manifest between 12 and 16 weekly sets per muscle group for most natural lifters. The steepest growth gains occur between 6 and 12 sets per week. Beyond 16 to 20 sets per week, the recovery penalty outweighs the marginal hypertrophic stimulus.

### Do warm-up sets count toward my weekly volume total?

No. Only working sets performed within 0 to 3 reps in reserve count toward your weekly volume target. Light warm-up sets prepare the nervous system, increase tissue temperature, and rehearse movement mechanics, but they do not generate sufficient mechanical tension on muscle fibers to stimulate hypertrophy.

### How should I count compound exercises for smaller muscles like triceps or biceps?

Compound movements provide direct stimulus to prime movers and secondary stimulus to synergists. For example, a barbell bench press counts as 1 direct set for pectorals, but roughly 0.5 fractional sets for triceps and anterior deltoids. If you complete 10 sets of heavy pressing movements in a week, your triceps have already accumulated roughly 5 indirect sets, meaning you only need 6 to 8 direct isolation sets (such as cable pressdowns) to hit your weekly target.

### How do I know if I am doing too much weekly volume?

The clearest indicators of excessive volume are declining strength on your opening lifts across consecutive weeks, persistent joint aches rather than muscular soreness, disrupted sleep patterns, and lack of motivation to train. If your logbook numbers are regressing despite adequate caloric intake, reduce weekly sets by 30 to 50 percent for a one-week deload before building back to a sustainable volume range.
