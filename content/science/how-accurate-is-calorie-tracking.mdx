Treat your calorie log as an estimate; review it alongside longer-term progress instead of cutting food after one unchanged weigh-in.

Two different questions matter: did you record what you ate, and how accurately does the entry describe that food? Adult food-record research and laboratory testing of restaurant meals illustrate both sources of uncertainty.

## Can successful weight-loss maintainers still underreport intake?

[Dahle and colleagues (2021, PMID 33742193)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8246606/) compared three-day food diaries with seven-day energy expenditure measured using doubly labeled water. The study included 26 weight-loss maintainers, 33 normal-weight controls and 32 controls with overweight or obesity.

Among maintainers, the median recorded intake was 605 calories per day below measured expenditure. That is a group result, not a correction to add to your own app. Individual errors varied, and some people reported more than their measured expenditure.

The authors did not establish that people were deliberately misreporting. Forgotten items, portion estimates and recording mistakes were possible explanations. Nor did this study test the accuracy of today's individual calorie-tracking apps.

<details>
<summary>Why weight stability matters in this study</summary>

Doubly labeled water measures expenditure, not food intake directly. Participants were weight stable during assessment, allowing expenditure to serve as an intake estimate. The maintainers had previously lost at least 13.6 kg and kept it off for at least a year. A small, relatively homogeneous case-control sample cannot establish a fixed error rate for every person or explain an individual weight plateau.

</details>

## Are restaurant calorie numbers exact?

[Urban and colleagues (2011, PMID 21771989)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4363942/) tested 269 food samples from 42 US restaurants. Fifty samples, or 19%, contained at least 100 calories more per portion than stated. Across the entire sample, however, the average measured-minus-stated difference was only 10 calories, with a 95% confidence interval from −15 to 34.

The useful distinction is between the average and a particular meal. Some portions differed substantially even though there was no statistically significant overall difference. That does not prove every label was accurate, and it does not justify automatically adding oil or a fixed percentage to every restaurant entry.

These foods were purchased in 2010 in three states. The study demonstrates portion-level uncertainty; it does not estimate the current error rate of every restaurant.

## How can you make a log more useful?

Treat these as practical ways to keep records comparable, not a clinically validated correction formula:

- Match the entry to the food and preparation: use a cooked-food entry for a cooked weight, or a raw-food entry for a raw weight.
- Record drinks, sauces and cooking ingredients as well as the main meal.
- Weigh portions when practical, especially when a vague serving description makes the estimate unclear.
- Mark restaurant meals as estimates rather than inventing precision.
- Review patterns over several weeks alongside weight trends, training, hunger and how you feel.

Consistency helps interpretation, but tracking error is not necessarily constant like a scale that always reads too high. An unchanged weight trend cannot, by itself, identify the cause. Persistent concerns deserve an individual review rather than an automatic calorie reduction.

## FAQ

### Should I add 605 calories to my food log?

No. That was the median reporting gap in one study group. It is not a personal adjustment rule.

### Should I weigh food raw or cooked?

Either can be useful if the database entry matches the state in which you weighed it. Switching between raw and cooked entries without changing the weight can introduce errors.

### What if I do not know a restaurant meal's calories?

Use available nutrition information or a reasonably similar food entry, record the portion as best you can, and keep the uncertainty in mind. There is no universal extra-oil allowance established by the cited research.

### Does a plateau prove I am logging badly?

No. A food log and a short weight trend cannot diagnose the cause. Review the records, the length of the trend and relevant health factors before changing your plan. If tracking becomes distressing, discuss a less intensive approach with a qualified professional.
